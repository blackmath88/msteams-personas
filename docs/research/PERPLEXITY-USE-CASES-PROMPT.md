# Perplexity research prompt — use cases

Copy the prompt below into Perplexity. This is a research request, not completed research.

Research realistic collaboration use cases for a visual Microsoft Teams scenario library.

PRODUCT CONTEXT
We are building msteams-personas: a library where someone can explore a stereotypical working situation, adapt it to their own context, and obtain:
1. Screenshot-like fictional Teams/M365 scenes: channels, posts, replies, files and tasks.
2. Conceptual setup maps: people, teams, channels, artefacts and relationships.
3. Principle explainers: why a decision or file belongs in one place rather than another.
4. Before/after comparisons and short teaching sequences.

Users should get those visuals without provisioning a real Microsoft tenant or creating Teams environments. This is a training, consulting and adoption-design tool. We need realistic working situations and meaningful choices, not a feature catalogue.

EXISTING MATERIAL
Our fictional higher-education collection already covers:
- A five-person administrative team plus a partner department organising Leadership Forum 2027.
- A seven-person urban-health research group collaborating on a manuscript and field campaign, with research data outside M365.
- An individual academic moving between personal work, occasional coauthoring, absence cover and recurring workshops.
- A commission with six meetings a year, papers, decisions and a few follow-up tasks.
- A three-person teaching team managing semester preparation and handover while keeping student-facing work on the official learning platform.

We want basic business use cases as well as higher education. Identify transferable patterns and differences; do not simply rename university people.

RESEARCH QUESTIONS
Which common working situations are understandable at a glance, valuable to explain visually, and sufficiently different to justify separate library examples? When should people use an existing workspace, a new Team, a channel, chat, a shared file, a simple list or personal tools? What remains outside Teams?

Investigate these as hypotheses, not predetermined recommendations: small department operations; cross-functional project; employee onboarding; customer onboarding or service delivery; recurring management/committee meetings; knowledge handover; external collaboration; individual-to-shared document work; event coordination.

METHOD
- Use current Microsoft primary documentation for product mechanisms and constraints. State the research date and distinguish current Teams behaviour from older UI guidance.
- Use credible case studies, practitioner evidence and research for workplace patterns.
- Label each substantive claim as Microsoft-documented mechanism, observed practice, or proposed synthesis.
- Cite sources beside claims with direct links and publication/update dates where available. Flag uncertainty, conflicting guidance and weak evidence.
- Distinguish technical capability from organisational recommendation.
- Check licensing, tenant-policy, external-access and membership assumptions only where they materially change a use case. Avoid broad compliance checklists.
- Do not invent case studies, statistics, sources or claims about University of Basel policy.
- Any technical claims about permissions, storage or access must have direct authoritative support.

DELIVERABLES
A. A comparison matrix of 10–12 candidate patterns across Business and Higher Education. Include: user-facing title, work goal, people/roles, size range, stable/project/recurring lifecycle, central friction, shared artefact, collaboration intensity, external involvement, teaching value and evidence strength.

B. Recommend six launch patterns total, with both basic Business and Higher Education represented. Explain distinctness and reuse of our existing examples. Prefer recognisable situations over specialist edge cases.

C. Develop the best four as fictional, implementation-ready scenario briefs. For each provide:
- Working-world identity and 3–6 named fictional people with roles.
- Trigger, stakes, work rhythm and personal/shared boundaries.
- Concrete current-state friction.
- 2–3 viable setup alternatives, trade-offs, contextual recommendation, and conditions under which that recommendation changes.
- What should stay outside Teams; what would be unnecessary structure.
- Three visual moments: current state, decision/principle, improved state.
- Enough sample content to render those moments: channels where justified, three short posts with replies, three file names, and up to four tasks with owner/status/due date where tasks are relevant.
- For each moment, describe both the screenshot-like scene and the conceptual explainer. Identify the learning point; keep participants and artefacts consistent.
- A facilitator question, a practical transfer action and likely misunderstanding.
- Technical dependencies or unresolved assumptions.
Clearly label fictional content and proposed synthesis separately from sourced findings.

D. A reusable taxonomy for library filters and templates. Separate working-world archetype, person/role, scenario, setup alternative and visual moment. Include search terms people would actually use.

E. A prioritised list of principles worth visualising: e.g. one shared document versus attachment copies, decisions visible beyond a chat, personal versus shared responsibility, workspace ownership and handover, appropriate attention signals. For each describe a visual metaphor or layout that explains the principle without relying on tiny screenshot text.

F. A short source/uncertainty register and the questions we should validate with trainers or users.

OUTPUT
Write the synthesis in English; use German (de-CH) for fictional scene copy and suggested library labels. Keep the executive recommendation concise, then provide structured tables and scenario briefs. Do not write application code or propose an AI backend.
